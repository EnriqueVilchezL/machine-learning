Part 1

The wisdom of the crowd

Many rough guesses, averaged, can be better than the best single guess.

Guessing the weight of an ox

Plymouth, England, 1906: about 800 visitors to a fair guessed the weight of an ox. Francis Galton studied the tickets.

True weight1198 lb
Average of all guesses1197 lb
Typical single guess off by0 lb

The errors point in different directions, so they cancel in the average.

Voting and averaging models

(a) Classification: each model votes for a class
(b) Regression: the predictions are averaged

Figure 3:An ensemble is a group of models whose answers are combined. It helps when the models are good and make different mistakes, as in Figure 2.

Combining as a formula

Regression: the ensemble predicts the average of the B models.

\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x})
(1)

Classification: each model votes and the class with the most votes wins.

\hat{y} = \underset{c}{\arg\max} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{I}\big(\hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x}) = c\big)
(2)

If each model has variance \sigma^2 and their errors are independent, the average has a much smaller variance:

\operatorname{Var}\big(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})\big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{B}
(3)
Example

Three models predict 200, 210 and 230:

\hat{f} = \tfrac{200 + 210 + 230}{3} \approx 213.3

With \sigma = 30 and B = 9:

\tfrac{30^2}{9} = \tfrac{900}{9} = 100 = 10^2

The spread drops from 30 to 10.

Only if independent

Shared mistakes do not cancel, as in Figure 2.

Bagging and random forests

One dataset, many slightly different copies, many trees.

Bootstrap samples

We only have one dataset. To get a new one, draw 12 examples from the 12 at random, putting each one back after drawing it.

Different examples used0
Left out0
Left out, on average—

Figure 4:Some examples repeat and about a third are left out. The left-out ones can serve as a free test set.

Who is left out

One draw misses a given example with chance 1 - 1/n. A bootstrap sample makes n independent draws, so the example is left out of the whole sample with chance

\Big(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Big)^{n} \approx e^{-1} \approx 0.368
(4)

The approximation is good once n is more than a few dozen. About 37% of the data is left out of each sample and about 63% is used.

Bagging trains model \hat{f}_b on sample b and combines the B models with equation (1) or (2).

Example

For the 12 examples of Figure 4:

(11/12)^{12} \approx 0.352

12 \cdot 0.352 \approx 4.2 examples left out on average.

For n = 1000:

0.999^{1000} \approx 0.368

Bagging: resample, train, vote

Figure 5:Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains one tree on each bootstrap sample. The trees are built independently, so they can be trained at the same time.

Random forests

With bagging alone the trees still look alike: the strongest feature, here the area, wins the top split in almost every tree.

A random forest lets each split choose only from a few features picked at random. With d features, a common choice is about \sqrt{d}.

The trees become more different, so their mistakes cancel better.

Figure 6:Top split of three trees that predict house prices. Each tree may only use the features shown under it.

More trees, smoother boundary

One deep tree follows every noisy point. A vote over many trees keeps the shape and drops the noise.

Part 3

Boosting

Models trained one after another, each one fixing what the last ones got wrong.

Learning from mistakes

Each round adds one very simple model: a single straight cut. Points it gets wrong become heavier, so the next cut pays more attention to them.

Round0
Combined accuracy—

Figure 8:AdaBoost on 50 points. Dot size is the weight; the dashed line is the newest cut and the shading is the weighted vote of all cuts.

AdaBoost formulas

Labels are y_i \in \{-1, +1\}. After M rounds, the cuts vote with weights \alpha_m:

F(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \alpha_m \, h_m(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \hat{y} = \operatorname{sign}\big(F(\mathbf{x})\big)
(5)

A cut with weighted error \varepsilon_m gets a larger say the fewer mistakes it makes:

\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2} \ln \frac{1 - \varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}
(6)

Mistakes (y_i h_m(\mathbf{x}_i) = -1) grow heavier and correct points lighter. Then the weights are rescaled to add up to 1.

w_i \leftarrow w_i \, e^{-\alpha_m y_i h_m(\mathbf{x}_i)}
(7)
Example

A cut with error \varepsilon = 0.2:

\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2} \ln \tfrac{0.8}{0.2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \ln 4 \approx 0.69

Weight of a mistake: times e^{0.69} \approx 2

Weight of a correct point: times e^{-0.69} \approx 0.5

A cut with \varepsilon = 0.5 gets \alpha = 0: a coin flip has no say.

Recall

Variance: the model changes a lot with the sample (overfitting). Bias: the model is too simple (underfitting).

Ensembles in practice

  • Random forests are a strong default for tables of data: good accuracy with little tuning.
  • Boosted trees often give the best scores, and they win many competitions on tabular data.
  • Costs: hundreds of trees are slower to train and to run, and much harder to explain than one tree.
  • Feature importance recovers part of the explanation: how much each feature helped across all the trees.

Key ideas

Remember
  • An ensemble combines the answers of many models by voting or averaging.
  • It works when the models are better than chance and make different mistakes.
  • Bagging trains trees on bootstrap samples; random forests also pick features at random.
  • Boosting trains simple models in sequence, each focused on the previous mistakes.
  • Bagging mainly reduces variance; boosting mainly reduces bias.

Part 1

Ask the neighbors

Keep every example and compare when a question arrives.

Lazy learning

Most methods learn a model and can forget the data. Instance-based methods keep the examples and do the work at prediction time.

To guess the price of a house, look at the most similar houses that were sold. That is k-nearest neighbors (k-NN).

The vote as a formula

Count, for each class c, how many of the k neighbors have that class, and keep the class with the most votes:

\hat{y} = \underset{c}{\arg\max} \sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} \mathbb{I}(y_i = c)
(1)

The indicator \mathbb{I}(y_i = c) is 1 when neighbor i belongs to class c and 0 otherwise, so the sum is a vote count.

\arg\max_c returns the class with the largest count, not the count itself.

Example

With k = 5: three orange and two blue neighbors.

Votes: orange 3, blue 2.

\Rightarrow \ \hat{y} = \text{orange}

Distance and scaling

Straight-line distance between two examples, one term per feature: d = \sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2 + (a_2-b_2)^2 + \cdots}

(a) Straight line 5; Manhattan 3 + 4 = 7
(b) Raw units: income in dollars dominates
(c) Both features scaled to 0–1

Figure 3:A one-year gap in age counts as much as a one-dollar gap in income, so in (b) the nearest example to Q is A. After min-max scaling (Topic 4) it is B.

Distance formulas

Euclidean distance: the straight line, adding one squared gap per feature.

d_2(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d} (a_j - b_j)^2}
(2)

Manhattan distance: walk along the grid, adding the absolute gaps.

d_1(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} |a_j - b_j|
(3)

Both are cases of the Minkowski distance, with p = 2 and p = 1.

d_p(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d} |a_j - b_j|^p\Big)^{1/p}
(4)
Example

For P = (1, 1) and R = (4, 5) of Figure 3a:

d_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5

d_1 = 3 + 4 = 7

Why scale first

Every term in the sum uses the raw units of its feature, so a feature measured in thousands drowns the others.

Choosing k with cross-validation

Try several values of k with 5-fold cross-validation (Topic 6) on the training data and keep the best.

With k = 1 training accuracy is always 100%: each point is its own nearest neighbor. Never choose k on the training score.

Weighted votes

A plain vote gives every neighbor the same say, even the far ones.

A weighted vote lets closer neighbors count more, for example with weight 1/d.

The result depends less on the exact value of k.

Averages and weights

Regression: the prediction is the mean of the neighbors' values.

\hat{y} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} y_i
(5)

Weighted vote: each neighbor counts with weight w_i, larger when it is closer.

\hat{y} = \underset{c}{\arg\max} \sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} w_i \, \mathbb{I}(y_i = c), \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)}
(6)

Weighted mean for regression: divide by the total weight instead of by k.

\hat{y} = \frac{\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} w_i \, y_i}{\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} w_i}
(7)
Example

Neighbors with values 2, 4 and 9, at distances 1, 1 and 2:

\text{plain}: \tfrac{2 + 4 + 9}{3} = 5

\text{weighted}: \tfrac{2 + 4 + 4.5}{1 + 1 + 0.5} = 4.2

Two practical limits

(a) Slow predictions: compare with every stored example
(b) Irrelevant features: noise columns blur the distances

Strengths and weaknesses

Table 2:k-NN at a glance.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Simple to explain: "look at similar cases"Slow predictions when there are many examples
No training step; new data is just addedMust keep all the training data in memory
Can follow borders of any shapeNeeds scaled features and a sensible distance
Works for classification and regressionHurt by irrelevant features and by many features
Only one main setting: kGives no simple summary of how features matter
When to use it

A good first baseline for small or medium data sets with a few meaningful, scaled features.

Key ideas

Remember
  • k-NN keeps the training examples and predicts from the k most similar ones.
  • Classification uses a vote of the neighbors; regression uses their average.
  • Similarity is a distance, so features must be on comparable scales.
  • Small k gives jagged borders and overfits; large k smooths too much. Choose k with cross-validation.
  • Prediction is slow on large data, and distances lose meaning with many or irrelevant features.

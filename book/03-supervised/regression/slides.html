Part 1

Fitting a line

A straight line that turns a feature into a predicted number.

Predicting a number

Regression predicts a number: a price, a temperature, a travel time. Classification predicts a category.

We draw the line that best follows the examples.

For a new house, we read its price off the line.

Figure 1:24 houses in the Central Valley (synthetic). Feature: area. Label: sale price.

Slope and intercept

\hat{y} = \text{slope} \cdot x + \text{intercept}

\hat{y} is the predicted price and x the area.

Slope: how much the price grows for each extra m².

Intercept: where the line starts, the price at 0 m².

Figure 2:The line of Figure 1. Learning means choosing these two numbers.

The line as a formula

With one feature, the intercept is \theta_0 and the slope is \theta_1:

\hat{y} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x
(1)

The residual of example i is the gap between its real label and the line:

e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - (\theta_0 + \theta_1 x_i)
(2)

A positive residual means the point lies above the line; a negative one, below it.

Example

Take \theta_0 = 20 (₡ M) and \theta_1 = 0.5 (₡ M per m²).

A house of 120 m²:

\hat{y} = 20 + 0.5 \cdot 120 = 80

It sold for 86, so its residual is

e = 86 - 80 = 6

Measuring the error

\text{error} = \dfrac{\text{sum of } (\text{actual} - \text{predicted})^2}{\text{number of houses}}

The cost function

The mean squared error (MSE) averages the squared residuals over the n examples:

J(\theta_0, \theta_1) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - \hat{y}_i\big)^2
(3)

It is the error shown in Figure 3. Each pair (\theta_0, \theta_1) is a different line and gets its own cost.

Learning means finding the pair with the smallest J.

Example

Four residuals: 1, −1, 2 and −4.

Squares: 1, 1, 4 and 16.

J = \tfrac{1 + 1 + 4 + 16}{4} = 5.5

The residual −4 alone gives 16 of the 22.

Why square the errors

Squaring makes every error positive, so errors above and below the line do not cancel.

It also makes big mistakes count much more: an error of 4 costs 16, an error of 1 costs 1.

Figure 4:(a) Each square has the error as its side. (b) Share of the total that each point contributes.

Least squares

The least squares line is the one with the smallest average squared error.

There is a known recipe that computes it in one step, so no guessing is needed.

Average squared error0

Figure 5:A poor first line moves toward the least squares line. Right: the error at each moment.

The least squares recipe

The slope compares how x and y move together with how much x moves alone:

\theta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}
(4)

The intercept then makes the line pass through the point of averages (\bar{x}, \bar{y}):

\theta_0 = \bar{y} - \theta_1 \bar{x}
(5)

These two values give the smallest cost J of equation (3). With several features the same idea is solved with matrices.

Example

Points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 7).

\bar{x} = 2, \ \bar{y} = 4

\theta_1 = \tfrac{(-1)(-2) + 0 + (1)(3)}{1 + 0 + 1} = 2.5

\theta_0 = 4 - 2.5 \cdot 2 = -1

Line: \hat{y} = -1 + 2.5x

Features and powers

With d features, each one gets its own coefficient:

\hat{y} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \theta_d x_d = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x}
(6)

The short form \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x} multiplies each coefficient by its feature and adds them, with an extra feature x_0 = 1 for the intercept.

Polynomial regression uses the powers of one feature as the features:

\hat{y} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \theta_2 x^2 + \cdots + \theta_p x^p
(7)
Example

The plane of Figure 6:

\hat{y} = 30 + 0.5 x_1 - 1.2 x_2

A house of 120 m² at 10 km:

30 + 60 - 12 = 78 (₡ M)

Degree 2

\hat{y} = 10 + 2x - 0.5x^2 at x = 3:

10 + 6 - 4.5 = 11.5

Scoring a regression

Average error: how far off a prediction is, in ₡.

R²: the share of the variation in prices that the line explains. 1 is perfect; 0 is no better than the average price.

Average error and R²

Mean absolute error: the average size of the residuals, in the units of the label.

\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big|y_i - \hat{y}_i\big|
(8)

R^2 compares the squared errors of the line with those of always predicting the average \bar{y}:

R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
(9)

The fraction is the share of the spread the line leaves unexplained, as in Figure 9b over 9a.

Example

Labels 2, 4, 6, so \bar{y} = 4.

Predictions 3, 4, 5.

\text{MAE} = \tfrac{1 + 0 + 1}{3} \approx 0.67

R^2 = 1 - \tfrac{1 + 0 + 1}{4 + 0 + 4} = 0.75

Assumptions and limits

Key ideas

Remember
  • Regression predicts a number; a line does it with a slope and an intercept.
  • The error is the average squared gap between actual and predicted values, so big mistakes weigh more.
  • Least squares picks the line with the smallest such error.
  • More features give one coefficient each; powers of a feature let the line bend, and too many overfit.
  • Check outliers, report average error and R², and do not trust the line far outside the data.

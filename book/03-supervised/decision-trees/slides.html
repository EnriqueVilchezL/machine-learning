Parts of a tree

Trees in computer science grow upside down: the root is at the top and the leaves at the bottom.

A new example enters at the root and goes down one branch per question until it lands in a leaf. The leaf gives the prediction.

Part 2

Choosing the best question

A good question leaves each side as pure as possible.

Pure and mixed nodes

A node is pure when all its points have the same class. The Gini impurity measures how mixed it is:

\text{Gini} = 1 - \left(p_{\text{blue}}^2 + p_{\text{orange}}^2\right)

Here p is the share of each class. Entropy is another score with the same shape.

Figure 4:Three nodes of 10 points and their impurity. Both scores are 0 for a pure node and highest at a 50/50 mix.

Impurity formulas

In a node with n_m examples, n_k of them of class k, the share of class k is

p_k = \frac{n_k}{n_m}
(1)

Gini impurity: the chance that two points drawn at random from the node have different classes.

G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2
(2)

Entropy: how surprising the class of a random point is, measured in bits.

H = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k
(3)
Example

The middle node of Figure 4: 7 blue, 3 orange.

p_{\text{blue}} = 0.7, \ p_{\text{orange}} = 0.3

G = 1 - (0.49 + 0.09) = 0.42

H = -(0.7 \log_2 0.7 + 0.3 \log_2 0.3)

\phantom{H} \approx 0.36 + 0.52 \approx 0.88

Extremes

Pure node: G = H = 0. A 50/50 mix: G = 0.5, H = 1.

Scoring a split

A question sends n_L points left and n_R right. Each side's impurity counts in proportion to its size:

G_{\text{split}} = \frac{n_L}{n_m} \, G_L + \frac{n_R}{n_m} \, G_R
(4)

The impurity decrease compares the parent node with its two children:

\Delta G = G_{\text{parent}} - G_{\text{split}}
(5)

The tree keeps the feature and threshold with the largest decrease. With entropy in place of Gini, the decrease is called the information gain.

Example

Parent: 5 blue, 5 orange, G = 0.5.

Left: 4 blue, so G_L = 0.

Right: 1 blue, 5 orange:

G_R = 1 - \tfrac{1 + 25}{36} \approx 0.28

G_{\text{split}} = 0.4 \cdot 0 + 0.6 \cdot 0.28 \approx 0.17

\Delta G \approx 0.5 - 0.17 = 0.33

Regression trees

When the label is a number, each leaf predicts the average of its training points. A good split makes the points on each side close to their average.

Leaf predictions

Classification: a leaf predicts its most common class.

\hat{y} = \underset{k}{\arg\max} \ p_k
(6)

Regression: a leaf m predicts the average label of its training points.

\hat{y}_m = \frac{1}{n_m} \sum_{i \in m} y_i
(7)

For regression the impurity of a node is the mean squared error around that average. Splits are scored as in (4), with this error in place of G.

\text{MSE}_m = \frac{1}{n_m} \sum_{i \in m} \big(y_i - \hat{y}_m\big)^2
(8)
Example

A leaf with 7 blue and 3 orange points predicts blue.

A leaf with labels 6, 8 and 10:

\hat{y}_m = \tfrac{6 + 8 + 10}{3} = 8

\text{MSE}_m = \tfrac{4 + 0 + 4}{3} \approx 2.67

Stopping and pruning

Stop early, while growing:

  • limit the maximum depth;
  • ask for a minimum number of points per leaf, for example 5;
  • skip splits that barely improve purity.

Prune, after growing: build a large tree, then cut the branches that do not improve the score on validation data.

Note

The depth, the minimum leaf size and the amount of pruning are hyperparameters, chosen with cross-validation (Topic 6).

Figure 8:A large tree. Press the arrow to prune two branches; each pruned node becomes a leaf.

Why no scaling

"Income ≤ 3" and "income in colones ≤ 1 500 000" split the points the same way. Changing units moves the threshold, not the tree.

Categories

A question can also be "is the color red?", with no need to turn categories into numbers first.

Key ideas

Remember
  • A tree predicts with yes/no questions, from the root down to a leaf.
  • Each split cuts a region in two with a vertical or horizontal line.
  • The best split leaves the two sides purest (Gini or entropy).
  • Leaves predict the majority class, or the average for regression.
  • Deep trees overfit: limit the depth, set a minimum leaf size, or prune.
  • Trees are readable but unstable; ensembles combine many of them.

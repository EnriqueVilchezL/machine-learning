Part 1

Counting and Bayes' rule

A probability is a share of a group. Evidence shrinks the group we look at.

Probability by counting

Of 100 training emails, 40 are spam. Before reading anything, an email is spam 40% of the time.

The word “free” appears in 24 of the 40 spam emails (60%), but only in 6 of the 60 others (10%).

Keep only the emails with “free”: 24 of those 30 are spam. The share jumps from 40% to 80%.

Figure 1:Each square is one training email. A ring marks the emails that contain the word “free”.

A frequency tree

The same 100 emails, split first by class and then by the word.

To answer “is an email with free spam?”, follow the two branches that end in “free” and compare their counts.

Figure 2:A frequency tree for the emails of Figure 1. Percentages on the branches are shares of the box on the left.

Updating a belief

\underbrace{P(\text{class} \mid \text{evidence})}_{\text{after}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{class})}_{\text{how likely the evidence is}} \cdot \underbrace{P(\text{class})}_{\text{before}}

Compute the right side for each class, then rescale so the classes add up to 100%.

Spam after “free”80%

Figure 3:Bayes' rule on 100 emails. Faded squares do not contain the word; icons are rounded to whole emails.

Bayes' rule

The chance of a class after the evidence is the prior times the likelihood, divided by the chance of the evidence:

P(c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{x} \mid c) \, P(c)}{P(\mathbf{x})}
(1)

The bottom adds the top over all classes. It only rescales, so the classes add up to 1:

P(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{c} P(\mathbf{x} \mid c) \, P(c)
(2)

This is the “rescale to 100%” step of Figure 3.

Example

An email contains “free”.

Spam: 0.6 \cdot 0.4 = 0.24

Other: 0.1 \cdot 0.6 = 0.06

Spam after “free”:

\tfrac{0.24}{0.24 + 0.06} = 0.8

A rare disease

1 person in 100 has the disease. The test finds 9 of 10 sick people, and wrongly flags 9 of 100 healthy ones.

Most positive tests come from the large healthy group.

A positive test raises the chance from 1% to about 9%, not to 90%. A small prior pulls the answer down.

Figure 4:1000 people, one square each. Rings mark the people with a positive test.

Part 2

The naive assumption

Treat every word as a separate piece of evidence, and learn by counting.

Why “naive”

Once we know the class, naive Bayes assumes the words tell us nothing about each other. Their evidence is multiplied.

This is false: “free” and “money” often come together, so their evidence is counted twice.

It still works well, because it only needs the right class to win, not exact probabilities.

Figure 5:The class points to each word. No arrows between words: that is the naive assumption.

The naive formula

Within a class, the features are treated as independent, so their chances multiply:

P(\mathbf{x} \mid c) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid c)
(3)

P(\mathbf{x}) is the same for every class, so it can be dropped. Pick the class with the largest score:

\hat{y} = \underset{c}{\arg\max} \ P(c) \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid c)
(4)

Many small numbers multiplied become too small for a computer. Adding logarithms gives the same winner:

\hat{y} = \underset{c}{\arg\max} \ \Big[\log P(c) + \sum_{j=1}^{d} \log P(x_j \mid c)\Big]
(5)
Example

An email with “free” and “money”:

Spam: 0.4 \cdot 0.6 \cdot 0.5 = 0.12

Other: 0.6 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 0.05 = 0.003

\Rightarrow \ \hat{y} = \text{spam}, with \tfrac{0.12}{0.123} \approx 98\%

In logs: \ln 0.12 \approx -2.12 beats \ln 0.003 \approx -5.81.

Training is counting

For each class, count the emails that contain each word and divide by the size of the class.

The prior

40 of the 100 emails are spam, so the starting belief is 40% spam.

A zero

“winner” never appeared in a normal email. The next slide shows why that is a problem.

The zero-count problem

A share of 0% multiplies the whole score of a class by zero. One word erases all the other evidence.

Add-one smoothing: add 1 to every count and 2 to every class size, as if each class had seen one extra email with the word and one without.

Figure 6:The new email “winner, meeting, tomorrow, report”, scored with the counts of Table 2.

Counting and smoothing

The prior is the share of training examples in each class:

P(c) = \frac{N_c}{n}
(6)

The likelihood of a word is the share of the class that contains it:

P(x_j \mid c) = \frac{N_{jc}}{N_c}
(7)

Add-one smoothing: 1 on top, and 2 below because a word is either present or absent. No share is ever 0.

P(x_j \mid c) = \frac{N_{jc} + 1}{N_c + 2}
(8)
Example

From Table 2:

P(\text{spam}) = \tfrac{40}{100} = 0.4

“winner” in other emails:

raw: \tfrac{0}{60} = 0

smoothed: \tfrac{0 + 1}{60 + 2} \approx 0.016

“winner” in spam, smoothed:

\tfrac{12 + 1}{40 + 2} \approx 0.31

Part 3

Numbers and practice

Bell curves for numeric features, and when naive Bayes is a good choice.

Numeric features

Gaussian naive Bayes fits one bell curve per class: its center is the class average, its width the spread.

Boundary0 cm
Chance of virginica0%

Figure 8:25 flowers per class. Each curve is scaled by its prior; the boundary is where the curves cross.

The bell curve formula

For a numeric feature, compute in each class the mean \mu_{jc} and the standard deviation \sigma_{jc} of feature j.

The likelihood of a value is the height of that class's bell curve at the value:

P(x_j \mid c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \, \sigma_{jc}} \exp\Big(-\frac{(x_j - \mu_{jc})^2}{2 \sigma_{jc}^2}\Big)
(9)

Values near the class mean get a high likelihood, values far away a low one. The rest of the model is unchanged: plug the heights into equation (4).

Example

A class with \mu = 5.5 cm and \sigma = 0.5 cm, and a flower with x = 5.0 cm.

The flower is one standard deviation below the mean.

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \cdot 0.5} \approx 0.80, \quad e^{-0.5} \approx 0.61

P(x \mid c) \approx 0.80 \cdot 0.61 \approx 0.48

Strengths and weaknesses

Table 3:What naive Bayes does well, and what to watch out for.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Trains in one pass: it only countsIts probabilities are often too close to 0% or 100%
Works with little training dataRelated features count their evidence twice
Handles thousands of words with easeNeeds smoothing to avoid zero counts
Easy to explain: each word's evidence is visibleA bell curve can fit a numeric feature badly
Rule of thumb

For text, train naive Bayes first. Keep a slower model only if it beats it on cross-validation.

Typical uses

Spam filters, sorting news or support tickets by topic, and telling positive from negative reviews.

Ranking, not exact numbers

Trust which class wins more than the exact percentage it reports.

Key ideas

Remember
  • Naive Bayes picks the class that makes the evidence most likely, weighted by how common the class is.
  • Bayes' rule turns a belief before the evidence into a belief after it; counting a group of 100 makes it easy to follow.
  • When a class is rare, even strong evidence can leave its probability low.
  • The naive assumption treats features as independent within each class, so their evidence is multiplied.
  • Training is counting. Add-one smoothing avoids zeros, and numeric features get one bell curve per class.

Part 1

From scores to probabilities

Squash any number into a value between 0 and 1.

A classifier, despite the name

Linear regression (Topic 9) predicts a number. Logistic regression answers a yes/no question.

Its output is a probability of “yes”. A rule then turns it into the final answer.

Figure 1:Two yes/no questions. The model gives a probability; 50% or more means “yes”.

Why a straight line fails

Code the answer as 1 (pass) and 0 (fail) and fit a straight line. The line keeps going: it predicts more than 100% and less than 0%.

An S-curve stays between 0 and 1. It is flat where the answer is clear and bends where it changes.

Figure 2:32 synthetic students. A straight line (orange) against an S-curve (blue).

The S-curve

The S-curve (sigmoid) turns a score into a probability:

p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-\text{score}}}
Fit to the data0%

Figure 3:Students of Figure 2. Here the score is steepness × (hours − position).

The model as a formula

First a linear score, as in linear regression:

z = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \theta_d x_d = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x}
(1)

Then the sigmoid squashes any score into a number between 0 and 1:

\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
(2)

The result is read as the probability of “yes”:

P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x})
(3)
Example

Figure 3 with steepness 1 and position 3 is \theta_0 = -3, \theta_1 = 1.

A student who studies 5 hours:

z = -3 + 1 \cdot 5 = 2

\sigma(2) = \tfrac{1}{1 + e^{-2}} \approx 0.88

Shape

\sigma(0) = 0.5. Large positive scores give values near 1, large negative scores near 0.

Learning the curve

Figure 4:Each gray segment is the gap between a student’s real result and the curve. Learning chooses the curve that makes the real results as likely as possible, so the gaps shrink.

The log-loss

With p_i = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x}_i), the cost averages a penalty over the n examples:

J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - p_i) \Big]
(4)

Only one term is active per example: -\log p_i when y_i = 1, and -\log(1 - p_i) when y_i = 0. A confident wrong answer costs a lot.

Gradient descent lowers J step by step. Each weight moves against the slope of the cost, with step size \alpha:

\theta_j \leftarrow \theta_j - \alpha \, \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i) \, x_{ij}
(5)
Example

A student who passed (y = 1), natural log:

p = 0.9: \ -\log 0.9 \approx 0.11

p = 0.1: \ -\log 0.1 \approx 2.30

In words

p_i - y_i is the gap of Figure 4. Large gaps move the weights more; when all gaps are small, learning stops.

Part 2

From probability to decision

A threshold turns a probability into a yes or a no.

Choosing a threshold

The test says “positive” when the probability reaches the threshold.

Accuracy0%
Precision0%
Recall0%

Precision: of those flagged, how many are sick. Recall: of the sick, how many are flagged.

Figure 5:60 synthetic patients, 20 sick. Bottom: the confusion matrix.

The decision rule

With a threshold t, the model answers “yes” when the probability reaches it:

\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sigma(z) \ge t \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
(6)

Since \sigma(0) = 0.5, the default threshold t = 0.5 is the same as checking the sign of the score:

\sigma(z) \ge 0.5 \iff z = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x} \ge 0
(7)

The points with \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{x} = 0 form the decision boundary: a straight line with two features, as in Figure 6.

Example

With z = -3 + x (hours studied):

t = 0.5: “pass” when x \ge 3.

t = 0.8: z \ge \log 4 \approx 1.39, so “pass” when x \ge 4.39.

The cost of mistakes

Table 2:The worse mistake decides where to put the threshold.

TaskWorse mistakeThreshold
Medical screeningSending a sick patient homeLow, for high recall
Spam filterHiding a real email in the spam folderHigh, for high precision
Free tutoring offerMissing a student who will failLow: an extra offer is cheap
Fraud alertBoth cost moneyIn between, from the two costs
Rule of thumb

50% is only a default. Choose the threshold from the cost of each mistake, using validation data, never the test set.

Lower threshold

More “yes” answers. Recall goes up, and so do false alarms.

Higher threshold

Fewer, surer “yes” answers. Precision goes up, and more cases are missed.

Size

Near zero

Odds and log-odds

The odds compare the chance of “yes” with the chance of “no”:

\text{odds} = \frac{p}{1 - p}
(8)

Undoing the sigmoid shows that the score is the logarithm of the odds:

\log \frac{p}{1 - p} = z = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \theta_d x_d
(9)

So one more unit of x_j adds \theta_j to the log-odds and multiplies the odds by e^{\theta_j}, with the other features fixed.

Example

p = 0.8: odds = 0.8 / 0.2 = 4, four to one.

Log-odds: \log 4 \approx 1.39

Figure 7

“Contains free” has weight 1.2:

e^{1.2} \approx 3.32

The word multiplies the odds of spam by about 3.3.

Key ideas

Remember
  • Logistic regression is a classifier: it outputs the probability of “yes”.
  • The S-curve squashes a weighted score into a value between 0 and 1.
  • Learning picks the weights that make the real answers as likely as possible.
  • A threshold turns the probability into a decision; set it from the cost of each mistake.
  • The boundary is a straight line: curved patterns need new features, and many classes need one-vs-rest.

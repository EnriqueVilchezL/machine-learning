Part 2

Similarity and groups

Close points belong together, so first we decide what close means.

Distance between examples

Every method in this chapter compares examples. The usual measure is the Euclidean distance, a straight line between two rows of the table:

d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{d} (x_{il} - x_{jl})^2}
(1)

A feature measured in large numbers dominates the sum. Standardizing each feature first (Topic 4) puts them on the same footing:

x_{il} \ \leftarrow \ \frac{x_{il} - \bar{x}_l}{s_l}
(2)

Here \bar{x}_l and s_l are the mean and standard deviation of feature l. Change the scale and the nearest neighbor can change.

Example

Clients as (age, income in thousands):
A = (25, 500), B = (60, 520), C = (27, 800).

Raw: d(A,B) \approx 40.3, d(A,C) \approx 300.0. B is closer.

Divide age by 15 and income by 150: d(A,B) \approx 2.34, d(A,C) \approx 2.00. Now C is closer.

Measuring compactness

The centroid of group k is the average of its members, feature by feature:

\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|} \sum_{i \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_i
(3)

The within-cluster sum of squares adds the squared distance of every example to its own centroid:

W = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{i \in C_k} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \rVert^2
(4)

Small W means compact groups. More groups always allow a smaller W, so W alone cannot choose K. Topic 16 calls it the inertia J.

Example

C_1 = \{(1,2), (3,2), (2,5)\}, so \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = (2, 3).

Squared distances: 2 + 2 + 4 = 8.

C_2 = \{(8,8), (10,8)\}, so \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = (9, 8); squared distances 1 + 1 = 2.

W = 8 + 2 = 10.

The silhouette

For example i, let a_i be its average distance to the rest of its group, and b_i its average distance to the nearest other group:

s_i = \frac{b_i - a_i}{\max(a_i, b_i)}
(5)

Figure 4:For the point at 0: a reaches its own group, b the other one.

s_i lies between −1 and 1. Near 1: well placed. Near 0: on a border. Below 0: probably in the wrong group. Average it over all examples.

Example

Points 0, 2, 6, 8 on a line; groups {0, 2} and {6, 8}.

Point 0: a = 2, b = \tfrac{6 + 8}{2} = 7, s = \tfrac{5}{7} \approx 0.71.

Point 2: a = 2, b = \tfrac{4 + 6}{2} = 5, s = 0.6.

By symmetry the average is \tfrac{0.71 + 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.71}{4} \approx 0.66.

Part 3

Fewer features and judging results

Shorter descriptions, unusual points, and how to check an answer nobody gave us.

Fewer features

Dimensionality reduction maps each example to a shorter list of numbers:

g : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^q, \qquad \mathbf{z}_i = g(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad q < d
(6)

A projection takes a weighted sum of the features along a direction \mathbf{w} of length 1:

z_i = w_1 x_{i1} + w_2 x_{i2} + \dots + w_d x_{id}
(7)

Figure 7:Each point drops onto the line (dashed). Its position on the line is z_i: one number instead of two.

Example

\mathbf{w} = (0.6, 0.8), length \sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} = 1.

\mathbf{x}_i = (2, 1) gives z_i = 0.6 \cdot 2 + 0.8 \cdot 1 = 2.0.

A good direction keeps the points spread out along the line. Topic 18 (PCA) finds the best one.

When to use it

To explore a new data set, to summarize it, or when labels are too expensive to collect.

Key ideas

Remember
  • Unsupervised learning sees only the inputs \mathbf{x}_i; there is no label y_i.
  • Its main tasks are clustering, dimensionality reduction and anomaly detection.
  • Everything rests on a distance, so scale the features first.
  • Good groups are compact inside and far apart; W and the silhouette measure this.
  • Any algorithm finds groups, even in noise; check results with several measures and with people.

Part 1

Learning to generate

Turn random noise into new examples that look like the data.

Discriminative or generative

A discriminative model learns P(y \mid \mathbf{x}): on which side of a border a point falls.

A generative model learns the data itself, so it can draw new points from it.

Recall Topic 27: a VAE decoder turns a code \mathbf{z} into an example. A GAN also maps noise to data, and learns that map with a second network as judge.

Counterfeiter and detective

A counterfeiter prints fake bills. A detective checks bills and says “real” or “fake”.

Each one improves against the other. The detective learns which details give a fake away; the counterfeiter fixes those details.

The game ends when the detective can only guess: the fakes look like real bills.

Figure 3:The analogy from the original GAN paper. G is the counterfeiter, D the detective.

Two networks

Both G and D are neural networks (Topic 23). G outputs an example; D ends in a sigmoid and outputs one probability.

Figure 4:Noise goes through G to give a fake sample. D receives real and fake samples and outputs the probability that its input is real. Its answer flows back to train both networks.

Part 2

The adversarial game

One value, two players: D pushes it up, G pushes it down.

The discriminator’s loss

D is a yes/no classifier: label 1 for real, 0 for fake. Its loss is the log-loss of Topic 11 for one example:

L = -\big[\, y \log p + (1 - y) \log (1 - p) \,\big]
(1)

A real example has y = 1 and a fake one has y = 0. One of each gives

L_D = -\log D(\mathbf{x}) \; - \; \log\big(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)
(2)

The loss is small when D says “real” to real data and “fake” to fakes. A confident mistake costs a lot.

Example

D(\mathbf{x}) = 0.9 on a real bill:

-\log 0.9 \approx 0.105

D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 0.2 on a fake:

-\log 0.8 \approx 0.223

L_D \approx 0.33

Fooled

If D said 0.7 to the fake: -\log 0.3 \approx 1.20.

How the generator learns

G wants D to say “real” to its fakes, so it wants D(G(\mathbf{z})) close to 1.

G never sees a real example. Its only signal is the slope of D at each fake: backpropagation runs through D into G, and only \boldsymbol{\theta}_G changes.

Each fake moves uphill on D, toward the real data.

Figure 5:One feature x. Blue: real data. Orange: fakes. Purple: D(x).

The minimax game

Averaging the log-loss over all data and all noise gives one value, V(D, G). D wants it large, G wants it small:

\min_{G} \max_{D} \; V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[\log D(\mathbf{x})\big] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)\big]
(3)

V = -L_D on average, so D raising V is the same as D lowering its loss.

G only appears in the second term. It lowers V by making D(G(\mathbf{z})) large.

Both logs are at most 0, so V \le 0.

Example

With D(\mathbf{x}) = 0.9 and D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 0.2:

V = \log 0.9 + \log 0.8 \approx -0.33

G improves and D now gives its fake 0.6:

V = \log 0.9 + \log 0.4 \approx -1.02

G pushed V down.

A weak signal at the start

Early on the fakes are bad, D rejects them, and D(G(\mathbf{z})) is near 0. There \log(1 - D) is almost flat.

The non-saturating loss

In practice G minimizes a different loss with the same goal, make D say “real”:

L_G = -\log D(G(\mathbf{z}))
(4)

It is the log-loss of the fake with the label flipped to 1: G is trained as if its fakes were real.

When D(G(\mathbf{z})) is small this loss is steep, so G gets a strong signal exactly when it is bad. It reaches its lowest value at the same place as \log(1 - D): D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 1.

Example

At D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 0.2:

-\log 0.2 \approx 1.61, slope -1/0.2 = -5

\log 0.8 \approx -0.22, slope -1/0.8 = -1.25

At 0.01 the slopes are -100 and about -1.01.

Where the game ends

For a fixed G, the discriminator that maximizes V is

D^*(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}) + p_G(\mathbf{x})}
(5)

G does best when its samples follow the data, p_G = p_{\text{data}}. Then D can only guess:

D^*(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2} \ \text{ for every } \mathbf{x}, \qquad V = \log \tfrac{1}{2} + \log \tfrac{1}{2} = -\log 4
(6)

At that point L_D = \log 4 \approx 1.39 and L_G = \log 2 \approx 0.69: the values the demo on the next slide settles at.

Example

At some \mathbf{x}, p_{\text{data}} = 0.3 and p_G = 0.1:

D^* = \tfrac{0.3}{0.3 + 0.1} = 0.75

Swap them: D^* = 0.25.

Equal values, 0.2 and 0.2: D^* = 0.5.

Part 3

Problems and progress

What goes wrong in training, how to judge the results, and what GANs became.

Mode collapse

G only has to fool D. Making one kind of example very well can be enough, so G may ignore the rest of the data.

Figure 10:Real data with two bumps. (a) The goal. (b) G covers only the left bump; D learns that the left side is suspicious. (c) G hops to the right bump, and the chase can repeat. In the demo of Figure 9 the spread also fell near 0 for a while.

Unstable training

The losses of a GAN track the game between D and G. They can swing forever, or look settled while the samples are still poor.

Figure 11:The GAN of Figure 9, run twice. (a) Small learning rates, the same run as the demo: the losses look settled by step 50, while every sample is almost the same number until about step 200. (b) Large learning rates: the G loss jumps from step to step and the spread keeps swinging. Variants such as the Wasserstein GAN change the loss to make training steadier.

Judging the samples

Good samples need two things: each one looks real (quality), and together they cover the data (variety).

People look at grids of samples. A common number is the FID: it compares the average and spread of image features of real and generated sets. Lower is better.

Figure 12:Three sets of nine generated faces, drawn by hand.

Conditional GANs

Give both networks a label y: G(\mathbf{z}, y) and D(\mathbf{x}, y). D checks that the sample is real and matches the label.

The label picks the digit; the noise picks the style.

Figure 13:A hand-made stand-in for a trained conditional generator on handwritten digits.

From blurry to realistic

In five years GAN samples went from small, blurry digits to large faces that are hard to tell from photos.

Figure 14:Simple drawings of the kind of result at each step, not real samples. DCGAN brought convolutional layers (Topic 25); StyleGAN feeds the noise in at every layer to control style.

Uses and risks

Figure 15:Generated examples can add training data, help artists and sharpen images. The same skill makes fake video of real people: use it only with consent, and support tools that detect fakes.

When to use it

When sharp, realistic samples matter most, mainly images, and you can afford to watch and tune training.

Key ideas

Remember
  • A GAN generates new examples by passing random noise through a generator G.
  • A discriminator D, trained with the log-loss, tells real examples from fakes.
  • The two play a minimax game and train in turns; G uses the non-saturating loss.
  • At the ideal end, p_G = p_{\text{data}} and D outputs \tfrac12 everywhere.
  • Watch for mode collapse and unstable training; judge the samples, not the losses.

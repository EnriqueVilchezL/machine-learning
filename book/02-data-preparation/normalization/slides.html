Part 2

Two common scalers

Min-max scaling and standardization: same idea, different reference points.

Min-max scaling

Shift and stretch each feature so that its smallest value becomes 0 and its largest becomes 1.

x' = \frac{x - \min}{\max - \min}
Example

Ages go from 18 to 80. A 45-year-old becomes (45 − 18) / (80 − 18) = 0.44.

The order of the people and the relative gaps between them stay the same. Only the units change.

Figure 2:Eight ages on the original line (top) and after min-max scaling (bottom). The youngest maps to 0 and the oldest to 1.

Standardization

Replace each value by how many standard deviations it is from the average. The standard deviation measures the typical distance to the average.

z = \frac{x - \text{mean}}{\text{std}}

Average age 45, standard deviation 12: a 57-year-old gets z = 1 and a 33-year-old gets z = −1.

Result

Every feature ends with average 0 and standard deviation 1. The shape of the data does not change.

Figure 3:300 ages. Subtracting the mean moves the center to 0; dividing by the std makes one unit equal one standard deviation.

Mean and standard deviation

The mean of feature j adds its n values and divides by n:

\bar{x}_j = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij}
(1)

The standard deviation is the square root of the average squared distance to the mean:

s_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2}
(2)

Standardization uses both numbers:

z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j}{s_j}
(3)
Example

Ages 33, 33, 57, 57.

\bar{x} = \tfrac{180}{4} = 45

Each age is 12 from the mean: s = \sqrt{\tfrac{4 \cdot 144}{4}} = 12

z = -1, -1, 1, 1

n or n − 1

Some books divide by n - 1 in (2). With many examples the difference is tiny.

Part 3

Outliers and skewed data

Real data has extreme values and long tails. Choose the scaler with that in mind.

One extreme value

Typical span, min-max—
Typical span, robust—

Robust scaling subtracts the median and divides by the IQR, the width of the middle half of the data. Neither changes much when one value becomes extreme.

Figure 5:11 typical incomes (blue) and one extreme income (orange). Move the slider and compare the blue spans.

Robust scaling

Robust scaling uses the median instead of the mean and the IQR instead of the standard deviation:

x'_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \tilde{x}_j}{Q_3 - Q_1}
(4)

Compare min-max scaling, written for feature j:

x'_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \min_j}{\max_j - \min_j}
(5)

One extreme value sets \max_j by itself and squeezes all other values toward 0 in (5). The median and the quartiles in (4) barely move.

Example

Incomes in thousands: Q_1 = 400, \tilde{x} = 500, Q_3 = 600. Income 700 gives \tfrac{700 - 500}{200} = 1.

Min-max with range 300 to 700 maps 500 to 0.5. One income of 4300 makes it \tfrac{200}{4000} = 0.05.

Taming a long tail

Prices and incomes have many small values and a few huge ones. Taking the logarithm turns multiplication into steps of equal size: 10, 100 and 1000 become evenly spaced.

Long right tail

Scale after the log transform, not before.

Figure 6:Prices of 400 synthetic houses. After the log, the mean and the median almost meet.

The log transform

Replace each value by its base-10 logarithm:

x'_{ij} = \log_{10}(x_{ij})
(6)

Multiplying a value by 10 adds 1 to its log, so a tenfold jump becomes a step of one:

\log_{10}(10\,x) = 1 + \log_{10}(x)
(7)

The log works only for positive values. When a feature contains zeros, add 1 first:

x'_{ij} = \log_{10}(1 + x_{ij})
(8)
Example

Prices 10, 100 and 1000 become 1, 2 and 3.

The gaps 90 and 900 both become 1.

With (8), a value of 0 becomes \log_{10}(1) = 0.

Part 4

Scaling without leakage

The scaler is part of the model, so it learns only from training data.

Fit on training data only

Data leakage

If the scaler is fitted on all the data, the test set has already shaped the training data and the test score is too optimistic.

Figure 7:The scaler learns its numbers from the training data (blue) and reuses them, unchanged, on the test data (orange).

Training numbers, new data

Let T be the training examples. The scaler computes its numbers over T only, and s_j the same way:

\bar{x}_j = \frac{1}{|T|} \sum_{i \in T} x_{ij}, \qquad \min_j = \min_{i \in T} x_{ij}, \qquad \max_j = \max_{i \in T} x_{ij}
(9)

A test value x_j is scaled with those same numbers, never with its own:

z_j = \frac{x_j - \bar{x}_j}{s_j}, \qquad x'_j = \frac{x_j - \min_j}{\max_j - \min_j}
(10)

A test value can fall outside the training range, so a min-max result below 0 or above 1 is normal.

Example

Training: mean 45, std 12. A test age of 69 gives z = \tfrac{69 - 45}{12} = 2.

Training ages 18 to 80. A test age of 85 gives x' = \tfrac{85 - 18}{62} \approx 1.08.

Key ideas

Remember
  • Features with large numbers dominate distances unless we scale them.
  • Min-max scaling maps each feature to the range 0 to 1.
  • Standardization gives each feature average 0 and standard deviation 1.
  • With outliers use robust scaling; with long tails try a log first.
  • Distance-based models need scaling; tree models do not.
  • Fit the scaler on the training data only, then apply it to the test data.
